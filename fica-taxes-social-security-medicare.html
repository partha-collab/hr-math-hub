FICA taxes explained: Social Security and Medicare

FICA is the payroll tax that funds Social Security and Medicare. It is the same for almost everyone, which makes it easy to calculate once you know the rules.

By the HR Math Hub team. Reviewed October 2026. General information, not tax, legal or financial advice.

Try it: Paycheck Calculator runs these numbers for you.

What FICA stands for

FICA is the Federal Insurance Contributions Act. It has two parts: Social Security (formally called OASDI, for old-age, survivors and disability insurance) and Medicare, which pays for hospital and medical insurance for people 65 and older and some younger people with disabilities.

The 2026 rates

TaxEmployee paysEmployer paysApplies to
Social Security6.2%6.2%Wages up to $184,500
Medicare1.45%1.45%All wages
Additional Medicare0.9%NoneWages above $200,000

Together, an employee pays 7.65% on wages up to the Social Security cap, and the employer pays another 7.65%. The 2026 Social Security wage base comes from the Social Security Administration: contribution and benefit base.

The Social Security wage cap

Social Security tax stops once your wages for the year reach the wage base. In 2026 the most any one employee can pay is $184,500 × 6.2% = $11,439. After that, your paychecks go up a little because that 6.2% stops coming out. Medicare has no cap.

Additional Medicare tax

Wages above $200,000 are hit with an extra 0.9% Medicare tax. Your employer starts withholding it once your wages pass $200,000 in the year, whatever your filing status. On your return, the real threshold depends on filing status: $200,000 for single and head of household, $250,000 for married filing jointly and $125,000 for married filing separately. Employers do not pay a matching share of this one.

What FICA does not use

  • No standard deduction. FICA is figured on your wages from the first dollar. The standard deduction only reduces income tax.
  • Traditional 401(k) contributions do not lower it. They reduce income tax, but they are still subject to FICA.
  • Pre-tax health premiums through a cafeteria plan usually do lower it. So do HSA contributions made through payroll.

Two jobs and FICA

If you work two jobs, each employer withholds Social Security up to the wage base separately, without knowing what the other paid. If your combined wages go over the cap, you may have paid too much, and the excess is credited back when you file your federal return.

If you are self-employed

Self-employed people pay both halves, a combined 15.3% (12.4% Social Security and 2.9% Medicare), through self-employment tax, and can deduct half of it when figuring income tax. That is a different calculation from the paycheck calculator, which assumes you are an employee.

How FICA shows up on your Form W-2

Your year-end Form W-2 reports FICA separately from income tax, and the boxes often have different numbers:

  • Box 1 is wages for income tax. It leaves out traditional 401(k) deferrals and most pre-tax benefits.
  • Box 3 and Box 4 are Social Security wages and the Social Security tax withheld.
  • Box 5 and Box 6 are Medicare wages and the Medicare tax withheld.

Example: someone earning $60,000 who puts $3,600 into a traditional 401(k) will see $56,400 in Box 1, but $60,000 in Boxes 3 and 5, because 401(k) deferrals are still subject to FICA. If your Box 4 is not 6.2% of Box 3 (and Box 6 is not 1.45% of Box 5), ask your payroll department to explain, unless you hit the Social Security cap.

How pre-tax benefits can lower FICA

Payroll deductions taken under a cafeteria (Section 125) plan, such as employee health insurance premiums, are generally exempt from both income tax and FICA. Using $60,000 of wages and $2,400 a year in pre-tax health premiums:

  • FICA wages: $60,000 − $2,400 = $57,600
  • FICA at 7.65%: $57,600 × 7.65% = $4,406.40, instead of $4,590 without the deduction
  • Saving: $183.60 a year for the employee, and the same again for the employer

This is one reason employers like offering these plans. Your own plan may differ, so check how each deduction is treated.

What FICA costs an employer

An employer pays its own 7.65% on top of your wages. For a $60,000 salary that is $4,590 a year, so the employer's real cost of the job is about $64,590 before benefits, unemployment insurance and workers' compensation. That is why total compensation is higher than the number on your offer letter.

When Social Security stops coming out of your pay

Someone paid $200,000 a year, every two weeks, earns $7,692.31 a paycheck. Their wages pass the $184,500 wage base around the 24th paycheck, so the last two paychecks of the year have no Social Security tax. Medicare continues all year, and the extra 0.9% starts once wages pass $200,000. A bonus early in the year makes the cap arrive sooner.

Common questions

Do I still pay FICA after I turn 65 or start receiving benefits?

Yes. If you keep working, you and your employer keep paying FICA on your wages, even if you are already collecting Social Security or are on Medicare.

Is FICA the same as income tax?

No. FICA funds Social Security and Medicare and is a flat percentage of wages. Income tax funds the general budget and uses brackets, deductions and credits. They are withheld separately and appear on separate lines of your pay stub.

Do bonuses and overtime have FICA withheld?

Yes. All regular and supplemental wages are subject to FICA. See how bonuses are taxed and how overtime is calculated.

Can I opt out of FICA?

Generally not. A few groups, such as some state and local government employees covered by a different retirement system, and certain students working for their school, may be exempt. If you think you qualify, ask your employer or the IRS.

Where it goes

Social Security pays retirement, survivor and disability benefits. The Medicare tax pays for Medicare hospital insurance. Your Social Security earnings record is built from the wages reported through FICA, which is why it is worth checking your my Social Security account on ssa.gov from time to time.

To see FICA on your own pay, open the paycheck calculator. The detailed rules are in IRS Publication 15 (Employer's Tax Guide).
