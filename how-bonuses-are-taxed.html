How bonuses are taxed

A bonus feels like it gets taxed more than regular pay, but most of that is withholding, not your final tax. Here is how it works.

By the HR Math Hub team. Reviewed October 2026. General information, not tax, legal or financial advice.

Try it: Bonus Tax Calculator runs these numbers for you.

Why bonuses look different

The IRS treats a bonus as supplemental wages: pay on top of regular wages, along with commissions, overtime pay and similar items. Employers have special rules for how much federal income tax to withhold from supplemental wages. The rules are in IRS Publication 15 (Employer's Tax Guide).

The two withholding methods

1. Percentage (flat rate) method

If the bonus is paid separately from regular wages, or the employer simply chooses, it can withhold a flat 22% federal income tax. If a person receives more than $1 million in supplemental wages in a year, the part above $1 million is withheld at 37%.

2. Aggregate method

The employer adds the bonus to the regular pay for that pay period, works out withholding on the total as if it were a single regular paycheck, and then subtracts the tax already withheld from the regular pay. The result can be higher or lower than 22%, depending on your W-4 and pay.

Your employer chooses the method. If you would like to know which one it uses, ask payroll.

Withholding is not the final tax

Withholding is only a down payment. When you file your return, the bonus is added to your other income and taxed at your normal bracket rates, so whether you end up owing more or getting a refund depends on how the withheld amount compares with the real tax.

Worked example: a $5,000 bonus

Use a single filer and the 2026 standard deduction of $16,100.

Salary $40,000Salary $80,000
Federal tax withheld on the bonus (22%)$1,100$1,100
Actual extra federal tax on the bonus$600$1,100
Difference at filing timeAbout $500 over-withheldAbout even
Social Security + Medicare (7.65%)$382.50$382.50

At a $40,000 salary, taxable income is $23,900, so the bonus is taxed at the 12% bracket and costs $600 in real federal tax, even though 22% was withheld. At $80,000, taxable income is $63,900 and the bonus lands entirely in the 22% bracket, so withholding is about right. Someone earning more, in the 24% bracket or above, can find that 22% was not enough.

State income tax and any state payroll programs come out as well, and those vary by state. Social Security and Medicare apply to the bonus too, subject to the Social Security wage cap.

Ways to keep more of a bonus

  • Put part of it in a traditional 401(k), if your plan allows bonus deferrals. That lowers income tax now.
  • Ask about HSA or other pre-tax options that your employer offers through payroll.
  • Do not change your W-4 just for a bonus. Flat-rate supplemental withholding does not use it. If you expect to owe at filing time, a tax professional can help you plan.

Common myths

  • "A bonus pushes all my income into a higher bracket." No. Brackets are marginal, so only the dollars above each threshold are taxed at the higher rate.
  • "I'm better off without the bonus." Never. After tax you always keep more than you would without it.
  • "22% is the rate I will owe." It is a withholding rate. Your true rate depends on your total income.
