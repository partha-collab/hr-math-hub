401(k) basics: limits, match and tax savings

A 401(k) is one of the simplest ways to save for retirement and cut your tax bill. Here is how contributions, the match and the limits work.

By the HR Math Hub team. Reviewed October 2026. General information, not tax, legal or financial advice.

Try it: 401(k) Calculator runs these numbers for you.

How it works

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement account. You choose a percentage of your pay to contribute, which goes in automatically from each paycheck. The money is invested, usually in funds you pick from your plan's menu, and grows until you withdraw it in retirement.

Traditional vs Roth

  • Traditional 401(k): contributions come out before income tax, so your taxable pay is lower now. Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as income.
  • Roth 401(k): contributions are made with after-tax money, so there is no tax break now, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

Not every plan offers a Roth option, and you may be able to split between the two. Our 401(k) calculator models the traditional type.

These are limits on your contributions. Employer contributions count toward a separate, higher annual limit. See the IRS: 401(k) contribution limits for the official figures, which are adjusted most years.

How an employer match works

Many employers match part of what you put in. A common formula is "50% of your contributions up to 6% of pay". Example: you earn $80,000 and contribute 6%.

  • Your contribution: 6% × $80,000 = $4,800
  • Employer match: 50% × $4,800 = $2,400
  • Total going in each year: $7,200

If you contributed only 3%, you would receive only half the match. Contributing at least enough to get the full match is usually the first step, since it is a return you get immediately. Ask HR for the exact formula, and whether the match is "true-up" at the end of the year.

Vesting

Your own contributions are always yours. Employer contributions may vest over time. Under a typical schedule you own 20% after the first year and 100% after five, or all of it after three years. If you leave before you are fully vested, you may forfeit part of the match.

Tax savings

With a traditional 401(k), the $4,800 above reduces your taxable income by $4,800. For a single filer earning $80,000, taxable income is $63,900 after the $16,100 standard deduction, which sits in the 22% federal bracket. The saving is about 22% × $4,800 = $1,056 in federal tax for the year, before any state tax savings. Social Security and Medicare tax still apply to the contributions.

What it could grow to

As a rough, hypothetical example: $7,200 added at the end of each year for 30 years, with a steady 6% return, grows to about $569,000. Real returns are never steady, and can be negative in some years, so use this only to see how time and consistent saving work together. The calculator also lets you add a starting balance.

Roth or traditional: a side-by-side example

Take the same $4,800 contribution for a single filer in the 22% federal bracket.

A common rule of thumb is that traditional contributions suit people who expect a lower tax rate in retirement, and Roth suits people who expect the same or a higher rate. Nobody knows future tax rates, so many savers split contributions between the two to keep options open.

Why the match is worth getting in full

Using the example above, a person earning $80,000 with a "50% up to 6%" match:

  • Contributing 3% ($2,400) earns a $1,200 match.
  • Contributing 6% ($4,800) earns a $2,400 match.
  • The extra 3% ($2,400) therefore brings an extra $1,200, an immediate 50% gain before any investment return.

That is why most guidance suggests raising your contribution to at least the full match before saving elsewhere, as long as your budget allows it.

Fees matter more than they look

Funds in a 401(k) charge annual fees, shown as an expense ratio, and plans may add an administrative fee. Using the earlier hypothetical of $7,200 a year for 30 years: at a steady 6% it grows to about $569,000, but if fees took 1 percentage point a year (5% net), it would be about $478,000, which is roughly $91,000 less. Compare the fees on your plan's fund list, as low-cost index funds are often among the cheapest options.

Catch-up contributions and Roth rules for higher earners

Under recent federal law, starting in 2026, employees whose prior-year wages from the employer were above a threshold (around $150,000) must make their catch-up contributions as Roth. Plans must allow this for the rule to work, so ask your plan administrator how it is applied. Employees under the threshold can still choose traditional catch-up contributions.

When you change jobs

  • Leave it in your old employer's plan, if the plan allows it.
  • Roll it into your new employer's plan or into an IRA. A direct rollover, where the money moves between institutions, avoids tax withholding.
  • Cash it out, which is usually the costliest choice: income tax plus a possible 10% additional tax if you are under 59½.

If you have a loan against your 401(k), ask what happens to it when you leave, as it may become due quickly.

Common questions

Can I contribute to a 401(k) and an IRA?

Yes. They have separate limits, though income and workplace-plan coverage can limit whether a traditional IRA contribution is deductible.

Does my contribution lower my Social Security or Medicare tax?

No. Traditional and Roth 401(k) contributions are both subject to FICA. See our FICA guide.

What happens if I contribute more than the limit?

If you go over the limit, for example by working two jobs with separate plans, tell one plan administrator promptly. Excess amounts generally need to be corrected by a deadline in the following year to avoid double taxation.

Withdrawing early

Taking money out of a traditional 401(k) before age 59½ usually triggers a 10% additional tax on top of regular income tax, with some exceptions. Many plans also offer loans. Check your plan documents before you borrow or withdraw.
