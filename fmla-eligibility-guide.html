FMLA eligibility guide: who qualifies?

The Family and Medical Leave Act gives eligible employees up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave. Whether you qualify depends on both your employer and you.

What the FMLA does

The federal Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) lets eligible employees take unpaid leave for certain family and medical reasons, keeps their group health coverage going, and generally returns them to the same or an equivalent job. The official source is the U.S. Department of Labor: Family and Medical Leave Act pages.

Step 1: Is your employer covered?

  • Private-sector employers with 50 or more employees for at least 20 workweeks in the current or previous calendar year.
  • Public agencies (federal, state and local government), whatever their size.
  • Public and private elementary and secondary schools, whatever their size.

Step 2: Are you an eligible employee?

You need to meet all three tests:

  1. 12 months with the employer. The months do not have to be in a row. Some older work periods, such as a break of more than seven years, may not count.
  2. 1,250 hours worked in the 12 months before the leave starts. That is about 24 hours a week on average. Only hours actually worked count, so paid time off, holidays and sick leave hours do not add to the total.
  3. The worksite has 50 or more employees within 75 miles. Employees who work from home are generally counted at the site they report to.

Our FMLA eligibility calculator checks all three and tells you which ones you meet. Example: someone with 14 months of service, 1,500 hours in the last year and 120 employees within 75 miles meets all three tests.

Step 3: Is your reason covered?

  • The birth of a child and bonding with the child (within 12 months of birth).
  • Adoption or foster placement of a child.
  • Caring for a spouse, child or parent with a serious health condition.
  • Your own serious health condition that makes you unable to do your job.
  • Certain needs arising from a family member's military deployment.
  • Caring for a covered service member with a serious injury or illness, for up to 26 workweeks in a single 12-month period.

How much leave

Up to 12 workweeks in a 12-month period for most reasons. The leave can be taken all at once, or in smaller blocks (intermittent leave) or by working a reduced schedule when it is medically necessary. Employers choose how to measure the 12-month period, such as a calendar year or a rolling period.

Notice and paperwork

  • Your notice: 30 days in advance when the need is foreseeable, such as a planned surgery or an expected birth. Otherwise, as soon as practical.
  • Employer response: within five business days of learning that leave may be FMLA-qualifying, the employer should tell you whether you are eligible.
  • Medical certification: your employer can ask for it, and you generally have 15 calendar days to return it.

Pay, benefits and your job

  • The leave is unpaid under federal law. Your employer may require you, or let you, use accrued paid leave at the same time.
  • Health insurance continues on the same terms as if you were working, and you still pay your share of the premium.
  • Job protection: you are generally restored to the same or an equivalent job. A narrow exception exists for certain highly paid "key employees" when restoring them would cause serious economic harm to the employer.

State laws can add more

Many states and some cities have their own family and medical leave laws, some with paid leave, smaller employer thresholds or shorter service requirements. Examples include California, New York, New Jersey, Washington and Massachusetts. If you do not qualify under the federal law, you may still qualify under a state law.

If you are denied

Ask your employer in writing why, and for the notice of eligibility. Concerns can be raised with the U.S. Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division or your state agency. This guide is general information, not legal advice or a determination of your rights.
