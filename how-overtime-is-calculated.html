How overtime is calculated

Federal overtime follows a simple rule, but the regular rate, the workweek and exemptions are where mistakes happen. This guide covers all three.

By the HR Math Hub team. Reviewed October 2026. General information, not tax, legal or financial advice.

The basic rule

Under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), covered non-exempt employees must be paid at least one and a half times their regular rate for every hour worked over 40 in a workweek. See the U.S. Department of Labor: Overtime pay for the official summary.

Two points are easy to miss. First, the 40 hours is counted per workweek, not per day, under federal law. Second, a workweek is a fixed, recurring period of 168 hours (seven 24-hour days). Employers choose when it starts, but they cannot average hours across two weeks to avoid overtime.

A simple example

Suppose you earn $25 an hour and work 48 hours in a week.

  • First 40 hours: 40 × $25 = $1,000
  • Overtime rate: $25 × 1.5 = $37.50
  • 8 overtime hours: 8 × $37.50 = $300
  • Total: $1,300

The regular rate is not always your hourly wage

The regular rate is all pay for the week divided by hours worked. It includes more than base pay. Non-discretionary bonuses (promised in advance, such as an attendance or production bonus), shift differentials and similar pay are included. Discretionary bonuses, gifts, expense reimbursements and pay for time not worked (such as vacation or holiday pay) are generally left out.

Example with a bonus: $20 an hour, 45 hours, and a $90 non-discretionary weekly bonus.

  • Straight-time pay for all hours: 45 × $20 = $900, plus the $90 bonus = $990
  • Regular rate: $990 / 45 = $22.00
  • Overtime premium (half the regular rate for each of the 5 overtime hours): 5 × $11.00 = $55
  • Total: $990 + $55 = $1,045

Without including the bonus in the regular rate, the overtime premium would have been only $50, so leaving it out underpays the employee.

Exempt and non-exempt employees

Not everyone is entitled to overtime. Employees who meet all of the exemption tests, a salary basis, a minimum salary level, and a job duties test (executive, administrative, professional, computer, outside sales and certain others), are exempt. A few things people get wrong:

  • Salaried does not mean exempt. Many salaried employees are non-exempt and must be paid overtime.
  • A job title does not decide it. What counts is the actual duties.
  • States may set a higher salary level than federal law, so check your state labor agency.

State rules can be stricter

When state and federal law both apply, the more generous rule for the employee wins. California, for example, requires overtime for hours over 8 in a day and double time at certain points. Alaska, Colorado and Nevada also have daily overtime rules in some situations. Our calculator uses a weekly threshold only, so people in those states should check their own state's rules.

Common overtime mistakes

  • Work done before or after a shift (emails, setup, closing tasks) that is not counted.
  • Meal breaks that are interrupted by work but still deducted.
  • Time rounded in the employer's favor, over many weeks.
  • Bonuses left out of the regular rate.
  • Comp time offered in place of overtime pay. Private employers generally cannot swap time off for overtime pay in a later pay period.

If you think you were underpaid

Keep your own record of hours, schedules and pay stubs. You can raise it with payroll first. You can also contact the U.S. Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division or your state labor agency. Under the FLSA, a claim generally has to be brought within two years, or three if the violation was willful.

This is general information only, not legal advice. Overtime law depends on your state and your job duties.
