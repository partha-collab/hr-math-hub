PTO accrual explained

Paid time off usually builds up a little each pay period. This guide explains the common methods, how caps and carryover work, and what happens to unused hours.

By the HR Math Hub team. Reviewed October 2026. General information, not tax, legal or financial advice.

Try it: PTO Accrual Calculator runs these numbers for you.

What accrual means

With an accrual policy, you earn PTO gradually as you work, instead of getting the full yearly amount on day one. Your balance grows with each paycheck, and drops when you take time off. Federal law does not require private employers to give paid vacation or sick leave, so the details come from your employer's policy and your state's law.

Three common methods

  1. Per pay period. You earn a fixed number of hours each paycheck: yearly PTO hours divided by pay periods.
  2. Per hours worked. You earn, for example, one hour of PTO for every 30 hours worked. That adds up to about 69 hours over a full-time year of 2,080 hours, and a part-time employee earns less.
  3. Front-loaded (lump sum). You get all your yearly hours on a set date, such as January 1 or your hire anniversary.

How much per pay period

After 10 biweekly pay periods, someone earning 4.62 hours each time has about 46.2 hours. If they have used 8 hours, the balance is about 38.2 hours. The PTO accrual calculator does this math, including carryover from last year and a balance cap.

Caps and carryover

  • A cap limits how much you can bank. When your balance hits the cap, you stop earning until you use some time. With a 160-hour cap, someone at 160 hours earns nothing more until they take time off.
  • Carryover rules decide how many unused hours move into next year. Some employers allow all of it, some a limited amount.
  • Use-it-or-lose-it policies, where unused hours disappear at year end, are not allowed in every state. California, for example, treats earned vacation as wages that cannot be forfeited, though employers may set a reasonable cap on accrual.

Is unused PTO paid out when you leave?

It depends. Several states, such as California, Colorado and Illinois, treat earned vacation as wages that must be paid at separation. In many other states it is up to the employer's written policy. If the policy says unused PTO is paid out, the employer generally has to follow it. Payouts are taxed as supplemental wages, a bit like a bonus.

Other things to check in your policy

  • Waiting periods: some employers do not let new hires use PTO until after 60 or 90 days.
  • Tenure tiers: accrual often goes up after years of service.
  • Part-time employees: accrual may be prorated by hours worked.
  • Sick leave vs. vacation: many states and cities require paid sick leave separately from vacation.
  • Leave and accrual: some policies pause accrual during unpaid leave, such as FMLA leave.

Checking your balance

Compare your pay stub or HR portal with your own count. If the hours earned each period do not match your policy, ask HR to explain the difference in writing. Keep a copy of the policy you were given when you were hired.
