Salary to hourly: how to convert pay

Converting a salary to an hourly rate is just division, but a few choices change the answer. Here is the formula, a table, and the traps to avoid.

By the HR Math Hub team. Reviewed October 2026. General information, not tax, legal or financial advice.

Try it: Salary ↔ Hourly Calculator runs these numbers for you.

The formula

Hourly rate = annual salary ÷ (hours per week × weeks per year).

A full-time job at 40 hours a week for 52 weeks is 2,080 hours. So a $60,000 salary works out to $60,000 ÷ 2,080 = $28.85 an hour. To go the other way, multiply the hourly rate by 2,080.

What changes the answer

  • Hours per week. If your real week is 45 hours, your effective hourly rate on a salary is lower. $60,000 ÷ (45 × 52) is about $25.64.
  • Paid time off. If your salary covers paid holidays and vacation, using 52 weeks is fair because you are paid for those weeks. Lower the weeks only for unpaid time off.
  • Shorter work weeks. At 32 hours (a four-day week), the divisor is 1,664 hours, not 2,080.

Common conversion mistakes

  • Using 4 weeks per month. A year has about 4.33 weeks per month, so 4 × 12 = 48 weeks leaves out four weeks of pay. Divide the annual figure by 12 instead.
  • Counting 24 paychecks. Pay every two weeks is 26 paychecks a year, not 24. Twice-a-month pay is 24.
  • Mixing gross and net. Compare the same kind of figure on both sides.

Comparing an hourly offer with a salary offer

Suppose one job pays $28 an hour and another pays $55,000 a year, both at 40 hours.

  • $28 × 2,080 = $58,240 a year
  • $55,000 ÷ 2,080 = $26.44 an hour

The hourly job is better on base pay by $3,240 a year, but compare the full picture. Hourly employees are often eligible for overtime pay. If the hourly job averaged three overtime hours a week at $42 (time and a half), that adds 3 × $42 × 52 = $6,552, for about $64,792 a year. Salaried roles, on the other hand, may come with more paid leave, bonuses or benefits, and a steadier paycheck if work hours dip. Retirement matches and health insurance costs are worth adding to either side.

Is a salaried employee always exempt from overtime?

No. Salary alone does not make someone exempt from overtime. See our overtime guide for the exemption tests. Hourly equivalents also help when you are deciding if a salary is fair for the hours you really work, and they matter for the federal and state minimum wage comparison.
