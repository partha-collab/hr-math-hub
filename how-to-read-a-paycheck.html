How to read a paycheck: every line explained

A pay stub packs a lot of numbers into a small space. Here is what each line means, where the money goes, and what to check each pay period.

By the HR Math Hub team. Reviewed October 2026. General information, not tax, legal or financial advice.

Try it: Paycheck Calculator runs these numbers for you.

Start with gross pay

Gross pay is what you earned before anything is taken out. For an hourly employee it is hours worked times the hourly rate, plus any overtime premium. For a salaried employee it is the annual salary divided by the number of pay periods. Many stubs also show earnings codes such as regular, overtime, bonus, holiday or PTO, so you can see how the total was built.

Two things are worth checking right away: the hours match what you actually worked, and the rate is the one you were promised. Errors here carry through every line below.

Pre-tax deductions

Pre-tax deductions come out of your gross pay before income tax is figured, so they lower the pay that is taxed. Common ones are:

  • Traditional 401(k) contributions. They reduce your federal and most state income tax, but they do not reduce Social Security or Medicare tax.
  • Health, dental and vision premiums paid through an employer cafeteria plan. These usually reduce income tax and Social Security and Medicare tax.
  • Health savings account (HSA) and flexible spending account (FSA) contributions taken through payroll, which generally get the same treatment.
  • Commuter benefits such as transit or parking, where your employer offers them.

Taxes withheld

Federal income tax

Your employer withholds federal income tax using the information on your Form W-4 (filing status, other jobs, dependents and any extra withholding you ask for) and the IRS withholding tables in IRS Publication 15 (Employer's Tax Guide). Withholding is an estimate of your tax for the year spread over your paychecks. It is not your final tax bill, which is settled when you file a return.

Social Security and Medicare (FICA)

These are payroll taxes. Social Security is 6.2% of wages up to a yearly cap ($184,500 in 2026), and Medicare is 1.45% of all wages, with an extra 0.9% on wages above $200,000. Your employer pays a matching amount on top. Read more in our guide to FICA taxes.

State and local tax

Most states tax wages, but Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington and Wyoming do not. A few cities and counties add their own income tax. Some states also take small payroll contributions for programs such as disability insurance or paid family leave, and those can show up as separate lines.

Post-tax deductions

These come out of what is left after tax. Examples are Roth 401(k) contributions, union dues, charitable giving through payroll, life insurance you buy on your own, and wage garnishments such as child support or a court order.

Net pay and year-to-date totals

Net pay, often called take-home pay, is gross pay minus all deductions and taxes. It is the amount deposited in your account. Next to each line you will usually see a year-to-date (YTD) column. YTD totals are handy for catching mistakes, and they show how close you are to the Social Security wage cap or your 401(k) limit.

A worked example

Take a single filer earning $60,000 a year, paid every two weeks (26 paychecks), with no 401(k) and no state tax. We use the 2026 federal standard deduction of $16,100.

LinePer yearPer paycheck
Gross pay$60,000$2,307.69
Federal income tax (estimate)$5,020$193.08
Social Security (6.2%)$3,720$143.08
Medicare (1.45%)$870$33.46
Net pay before state tax$50,390$1,938.07

The federal figure comes from taxing $43,900 (the $60,000 less the $16,100 standard deduction): 10% on the first $12,400 and 12% on the rest. If this person put 6% of pay ($3,600 a year) into a traditional 401(k), taxable income would fall to $40,300 and federal tax to $4,588, a saving of $432, while Social Security and Medicare would stay the same.

You can try your own numbers in the paycheck calculator. Your real stub can differ because of your W-4, benefits, local taxes and state programs.

What to check every pay period

  1. Hours, rate and any overtime match your own records.
  2. The pay period dates and pay date are right.
  3. Deductions you did not agree to, or that changed without notice.
  4. Your 401(k) and benefit amounts match what you signed up for.
  5. YTD gross pay is rising by about the right amount each time.

If something looks wrong, ask your payroll or HR team to explain the line, in writing if possible. If it is a tax question, a tax professional can look at your whole situation. Our contact page explains what we can and cannot help with.
